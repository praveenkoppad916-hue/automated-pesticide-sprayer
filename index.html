01 / Project overview

Engineering a practical approach to farm spraying.

Traditional backpack and hand-operated sprayers demand physical effort and close operator proximity to the spray zone. This Bachelor of Engineering project examined how a compact mobile platform could coordinate movement and liquid delivery using accessible electromechanical components.

THE CHALLENGE

Manual effort

Repetitive carrying, pumping and walking can make small-farm spraying labor-intensive. Changes in walking speed and nozzle position can also affect consistency.

DESIGN INTENT

Controlled application

Investigate a tank, pump and nozzle arrangement mounted on a wheeled chassis, with coordinated movement and spray activation.

ENGINEERING PRIORITY

Safe operation

Consider electrical isolation, stable mounting, chemical compatibility, operator distance and a stop state that disables motion and spray.

02 / System architecture

Three systems. One coordinated platform.

The reference architecture separates control signals, liquid flow and electrical power. This makes the concept easier to understand, troubleshoot and improve.

Architecture shown is an engineering reconstruction for presentation, not a verified schematic from the original build.

Mechanical assembly

A low-center-of-gravity chassis, suitable wheels, tank restraints and adjustable nozzle supports help address balance, ground clearance and repeatable spray positioning.

Electrical assembly

Motor-drive circuitry, protected battery wiring and a separate pump switching path allow motion and liquid delivery to be controlled independently.

Reference operating workflow

Inspect tank, plumbing, battery, electrical connections and stop controls.

Initialize the system with motion and pump outputs disabled.

Enable controlled travel and spray only when operating conditions are suitable.

Disable the pump before stopping, turning or entering a restricted area.

Safety interlocks to consider

  • Emergency-stop input removes drive and pump output.
  • Pump is disabled during unsuitable operating conditions.
  • Low-voltage or electrical fault produces a safe shutdown.
  • People, animals and bystanders must be clear of the spray zone.
  • Field spraying must follow product labels and local requirements.
05 / Engineering analysis

Design choices backed by calculations.

Spray uniformity depends on more than switching a pump on. Flow rate, travel speed and effective coverage width interact to determine the theoretical application volume.

Why the variables matter

Flow rate

Increasing total nozzle flow increases liquid delivered per unit area at constant speed and width.

Ground speed

Faster travel reduces application volume per area if nozzle flow stays unchanged.

Spray width

Coverage width depends on nozzle pattern, spacing, height and overlap.

Real-world constraints

Pressure changes, nozzle wear, wind, vibration and terrain affect practical delivery.

The calculator demonstrates a standard ground-sprayer engineering relationship. It is not a measurement from the original student project.
STRUCTURAL

Stability

Keep the tank secure and low to reduce tipping risk, particularly with changing liquid level and uneven ground.

ELECTRICAL

Power budget

Size battery, wiring, switching devices and fuses against realistic pump and motor current demands.

HYDRAULIC

Flow consistency

Account for nozzle choice, pump characteristics, filtering and pressure variation when evaluating delivery.

06 / Verification methodology

How the design can be tested.

A structured test plan connects engineering assumptions to observable system behavior. The following procedures are proposed verification activities, not claims of recorded historical results.

Bench validation

  • Check wiring polarity, fuses and isolation switch.
  • Verify motor directions and response to stop commands.
  • Use clean water to inspect pump, hose and nozzle leakage.
  • Measure flow over a timed interval with a graduated container.

Controlled mobility trials

  • Observe traction and turning on a safe test surface.
  • Check tank restraint and vehicle stability.
  • Assess the effect of battery discharge on operation.
  • Verify that stopping motion disables spraying as intended.

Spray pattern evaluation

  • Compare nozzle coverage at a fixed test height.
  • Inspect distribution using clean-water test methods.
  • Check for drift-prone conditions and overspray.
  • Review repeatability across multiple passes.

Failure-mode checks

  • Blocked filter or nozzle.
  • Loose hose connection.
  • Motor stall or overloaded drive electronics.
  • Low supply voltage and emergency shutdown.
07 / Project documentation

Engineering research, presented responsibly.

This Bachelor of Engineering project investigated automated crop-spraying concepts through mechanical, electrical and fluid-system design considerations.

Project scope

The website documents the problem, a plausible electromechanical architecture, representative subsystems, control logic, engineering relationships and a verification methodology. It is intended to communicate the design thinking and technical principles behind the undergraduate project.

The original build report and detailed prototype records are not currently available. Hardware choices, diagrams and examples presented here are reconstructed engineering illustrations rather than verified historical measurements.

Research and professional relevance

The project demonstrates engagement with mechanical design, electromechanical integration, basic automation, hydraulic delivery and practical agricultural engineering.

It also reflects transferable engineering habits: breaking complex systems into subsystems, evaluating constraints, documenting operating logic and designing for safe maintainability.
